The context

From Jerusalem to Rome

In 66 CE the province of Judaea rose against Rome. Nero sent the general Vespasian to crush the revolt. In 69 CE Vespasian left the war to fight for the throne in a Roman civil war, and he won it. His son Titus took over the siege of Jerusalem. In the summer of 70 CE the Romans broke into the city and the Temple burned. Jewish tradition dates the destruction to the ninth of Av, the same date that it gives for the fall of the First Temple.

In 71 CE Vespasian and Titus held a joint triumph in Rome. The historian Josephus watched it. He describes soldiers, captives, moving stages that showed scenes of the war, and the treasures of the Temple. Ten years later Titus was dead, and his brother Domitian raised this arch for him on the Via Sacra.

The arch shows a defeat from the side of the winner. This site reads it in two ways. The first reading asks how Rome turned the fall of Jerusalem into images of power. The second asks how Jews answered those images across two thousand years. Historians call the second kind of question reception: the study of how later people make their own meaning from a work.

Reading the dedication

Select a word to translate it. Latin capitals use V for both U and V. The dots are word dividers.

SENATVS POPVLVSQVE ROMANVS DIVO TITO DIVI VESPASIANI F VESPASIANO AVGVSTO

"The Senate and the Roman People, to the deified Titus Vespasian Augustus, son of the deified Vespasian."

The text never names Judaea, Jerusalem or the war.

A second arch for Titus stood at the east end of the Circus Maximus. It is lost, but a copy of its text, made in the Middle Ages, survives. That text honors Titus because, under the direction of his father, he conquered the Jewish people and destroyed Jerusalem, a city that every general, king and people before him had either attacked without success or never tried to attack.

The boast is false. Pompey captured Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon destroyed it in 586 BCE. The arch on the Via Sacra keeps silent about the war in words and lets its reliefs speak.

Under or around?

Visitors to the Forum can still walk through the passage or step around the arch. Choose a path.

To walk through is to take the road of the triumph and to pass beneath the deified Titus in the vault. Most visitors do it without a thought. For the Jews of Rome it meant to join the procession that carried the menorah away, and for that reason the custom said: go around.

To walk around is to refuse the triumph. The custom treats the arch as a place of mourning. It keeps the loss of the Temple in the body of every person who takes the longer path.

In 1948, accounts tell, Roman Jews answered the arch in a new way. After the State of Israel was declared, they walked through it from west to east, against the route of the triumph.

About the model

The 3D model is built in code with three.js. Its proportions follow the published measurements of the arch. The architecture, the Composite capitals and the coffered vault follow the real design.

The reliefs are simplified carvings. They follow the composition of the originals, but they are not exact copies, and the faces and details are generic. The ochre paint on the menorah follows the 2012 scan. The line between ancient marble and restored travertine is approximate.

The setting is a sketch. The Colosseum, the bell tower of Santa Francesca Romana and the Basilica of Maxentius stand in their rough directions at reduced detail.
